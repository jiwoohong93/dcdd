Overview

Discrete-token vision-language models such as Janus-Pro generate images by predicting sequences of codebook indices and decoding them with a VQ-VAE decoder. This design unifies language and image modeling, but visual fidelity remains limited by the discrete-token decoding stage, often resulting in distorted text, facial artifacts, and blurred textures even when the generated semantics are correct. Prior approaches address this limitation by replacing discrete tokenizers with continuous or hybrid representations, or by integrating diffusion into the generation pipeline. However, these approaches require retraining or realigning the VLM itself.

DCDD (Distribution-Conditioned Diffusion Decoder) takes a different route. Before sampling each image token, the VLM computes a full logit distribution over the codebook, but standard decoding retains only the sampled index, discarding the richer distributional information. These distributions capture the VLM's relative preferences across multiple plausible visual tokens, providing richer information than any single sampled index. We show that this discarded information provides a valuable conditioning signal for a diffusion decoder. At inference, DCDD converts the VLM's pre-sampling logits into distribution-weighted code vectors to condition the diffusion decoder. During training, proxy logits derived from the VQ-VAE encoder are calibrated to approximate the statistical properties of the VLM's logits, eliminating the need for VLM forward passes over the training dataset. The VLM remains frozen, the diffusion decoder is trained solely on ImageNet-1K, and it can optionally replace the native VQ-VAE decoder without modifying the VLM. On Janus-Pro 7B, DCDD reduces reconstruction FID by 74% and MJHQ-30K generation FID by 34%, while preserving performance on GenEval, DPG-Bench, and WISE.

Motivation

Three observations motivate the approach.

1

Fidelity is limited by discrete-token reconstruction

Encoding an image into discrete tokens and reconstructing it without the VLM already introduces visible losses in text, faces, and textures. This suggests that improving the decoding pipeline can enhance visual fidelity without retraining the VLM.

Detail loss occurs even without the VLM
2

The VLM knows more than the token it samples

Before sampling, the VLM assigns meaningful probability to multiple codebook entries, rather than concentrating entirely on a single token. Standard decoding retains only the sampled index, discarding these additional preferences.

Informative probability mass beyond the sampled token
3

Condition the decoder on the full distribution

Instead of conditioning on a single token index, DCDD uses a probability-weighted combination of codebook vectors along with distributional uncertainty features to guide a diffusion decoder.

No change to the VLM or its tokenizer

Method

Only the diffusion decoder is trained. Each of the three components answers one practical question.

LCDM · Logit-to-Code Distributional Mapping

How do logits become a conditioning signal?

A softmax over the codebook turns each token's logits into a probability-weighted mixture of codebook embeddings. A few scalar uncertainty features preserve how peaked or spread the distribution is.

LC · Logit Calibration

How do we train without running the VLM on every image?

Training uses proxy logits from the VQ-VAE encoder, which are far flatter than the VLM's. A gradient-free calibration aligns their distributional statistics with those of the VLM's logits, reducing the conditioning mismatch between training and inference.

Why it matters. Logit calibration enables decoder training using only VQ-VAE encoder outputs, without running the frozen VLM over the training dataset.

DD · Diffusion Decoder

What renders the final image?

A pre-trained FLUX.1-Fill model fine-tuned to render images from the code vectors and uncertainty maps. The decoder uses only VLM-derived token distributions, without direct text conditioning, to enhance visual fidelity while preserving the VLM's intended semantics.

Cross-domain generalization. The decoder is trained on ImageNet-1K only and never sees MJHQ-30K, so the MJHQ rows measure transfer to a different image distribution. Semantic benchmarks (GenEval, DPG-Bench, WISE) are preserved rather than traded for fidelity.

Computational overhead. At 30 denoising steps, DCDD adds 31% end-to-end latency (11.9 s → 15.6 s per image on an A100) in exchange for the 34% gFID reduction above. With 10 steps the overhead drops to about 11% while reconstruction still outperforms the native decoder on every metric. In both settings the frozen VLM accounts for most of the inference time.

Transferability across VLM scales. The diffusion decoder trained for Janus-Pro 7B can be reused with Janus-Pro 1B, which shares the same tokenizer and exhibits similarly structured token distributions, without additional decoder training (MJHQ-30K Animals gFID 35.14 → 26.09).

Why does DCDD work?

The improvement comes not only from diffusion-based decoding, but also from preserving the VLM's distributional information.

Conditioning on the full distribution improves generation fidelity beyond what diffusion-based decoding alone achieves: a further 15% reduction in gFID and 36% in ImageNet-1K rFID relative to the argmax-conditioned decoder.

2. Information beyond the top-1 token matters

Using only the top-2 codebook entries recovers 76% of the improvement from argmax to full-distribution conditioning, consistent with our Otsu-based analysis showing that capable VLMs assign meaningful probability to multiple tokens.

3. Better fidelity without reinterpreting the prompt

Compared with image-space refinement using FLUX.1-Kontext on the VQ-VAE output, DCDD achieves lower gFID (9.42 vs. 10.67) and higher CLIP-I consistency with the VLM's intended content (0.909 vs. 0.875).
